# Student Competitions That Produce Real Work

## A BlueRobins Field Guide for the 2026–27 Season

Every competition in this guide meets one standard: a student cannot win it in an afternoon.
Each one requires a project built over weeks or months, guided by a mentor, that ends in
something real — a research paper, a working prototype, an app, a documentary, a business
plan, a portfolio, an experiment that flies.

That standard is deliberate. Timed tests and one-sitting contests measure what a student
already knows. Project competitions build what a student can do. The difference shows up in
the work itself, and it is the difference admissions readers, scholarship committees and
research supervisors have learned to look for.

**39 competitions across 12 sectors, from grade 3 through grade 12.**

---

## How to read this guide

Competitions are grouped by grade band, because eligibility is the first filter any family
applies. Within each band, entries are ordered roughly by the depth of work they demand.

Every entry answers the same eight questions:

- **Sector** — the field the work sits in, for matching a student to the right arena
- **Who it's for** — exact grade or age eligibility
- **What students produce** — the artifact that comes out the other end
- **What it builds** — the capability the student carries forward
- **Recognition** — selectivity, awards and what winning actually confers
- **Key dates** — the 2026–27 season
- **Investment** — full cost, including the parts that arrive later
- **Where a mentor fits** — how outside guidance is treated by the rules

### A note on the younger bands

Nothing a third- or seventh-grader enters appears on a college application, and families
should never be told otherwise. What the elementary and middle school competitions do is
build the specific capability the high school competitions reward — the habit of keeping a
design logbook, the discipline of an abstract, the experience of defending your work to a
judge who asks hard questions.

That is why every entry for grades 3–8 in this guide names **where it leads**: the specific
9–12 program, usually run by the same organization, that the younger competition feeds. A
student who does FIRST LEGO League in fourth grade and FIRST Tech Challenge in eighth is not
collecting credentials. They are eight years into a discipline by the time it counts.

---

## What this guide excludes, and why

Spelling bees, quiz bowl, math league, geography bee and timed subject olympiads are
excellent activities and appear nowhere in this guide. They produce no artifact, and there
is no project for a mentor to guide. A student's performance in them is essentially fixed
before the day begins.

Also excluded: any award where every entrant receives a certificate, where a nomination
carries a fee, or where publication is contingent on payment. These are widely marketed to
families and widely discounted by the people they are meant to impress.

Every competition that follows survives both filters.

### 1. Invention Convention Worldwide

**The country's largest invention-education pipeline, and the only one that runs unbroken
from kindergarten through grade 12.** Students identify a problem they have personally
encountered, invent something that solves it, and defend the invention to judges who score
the *process* more heavily than the product.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Invention & Product Design |
| **Who it's for** | Grades K–12. Teams up to four; a team competes in the grade band of its oldest member |
| **What students produce** | A working invention or prototype, an inventor's logbook documenting the whole design process, a display board, and a recorded presentation |
| **What it builds** | Structured invention methodology — problem identification, prior-art search, iteration, and the habit of documenting decisions as you make them. The logbook is the same instrument professional engineers and patent attorneys rely on |
| **Recognition** | Judged on a published rubric: Invention Process (40 points), Invention Impact, Inventor Communication, plus an independent originality score. Students advance from local to state affiliate to U.S. Nationals, and on to Invention Convention Globals. Patent support and cash awards are offered by many affiliates |
| **Key dates** | Affiliate competitions run through the winter and spring. **U.S. Nationals: 9–11 June 2027**, at The Henry Ford in Dearborn, Michigan |
| **Investment** | Affiliate-level participation is often free through a school. Nationals costs **$365 per participant and $390 per accompanying guest**, plus travel and lodging; a parent or guardian must attend |
| **Where a mentor fits** | Mentoring is welcome throughout the build. The presentation itself must be the student's own: grades 3–4 may use notecards but receive no adult prompting of any kind; grades 5–12 use neither |
| **Where it leads** | Directly into the same competition at grades 6–8 and 9–12, then Globals — the cleanest continuous pathway in this guide |
| **Official site** | https://inhub.thehenryford.org/icw/home |

### 2. FIRST LEGO League

**The most recognized entry point into competitive robotics worldwide** — and a program in
transition. Teams build and program a robot while separately researching a real-world
problem and pitching a solution, so the season trains engineering and communication at once.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Robotics & Engineering |
| **Who it's for** | 2026–27 runs in two editions. **Founders Edition:** Explore, ages 6–10; Challenge, ages 9–16. **Future Edition:** a K–2 / ages 5–7 option and a grades 3–8 / ages 8–16 option built on LEGO Education Computer Science & AI kits |
| **What students produce** | A competition robot, the code that drives it, and an **Innovation Project** — original research into a community problem, presented to judges |
| **What it builds** | Iterative engineering, teamwork under a deadline, and early public presentation of technical work. The Innovation Project is most students' first experience of research that ends in a recommendation rather than a grade |
| **Recognition** | Regional and state tournaments with advancement; judged awards for Core Values, Innovation Project and Robot Design |
| **Key dates** | 2026–27 season game: **BIOGLOW**. Team registration runs through the season; tournaments are held from late autumn into spring |
| **Investment** | Team registration, plus robot kit and tournament travel. Fees increased for 2026–27; FIRST publishes current pricing and offers team grants |
| **Where a mentor fits** | An adult coach is structural to the program — mentoring is the expected model, not an exception |
| **Where it leads** | FIRST Tech Challenge (grades 7–12) and FIRST Robotics Competition (grades 9–12), both of which continue unchanged |
| **Worth knowing** | **2026–27 is the final season of FIRST LEGO League.** The FIRST and LEGO Education partnership concludes after BIOGLOW, and LEGO Education launches a successor program, **LEGO League, in August 2027**. Families starting now should understand they are joining a final season with a named continuation — not a program disappearing |
| **Official site** | https://www.firstinspires.org/programs/fll/ · Successor: https://education.lego.com/en-us/lego-league/program-update/ |

### 3. Toshiba/NSTA ExploraVision

**Now in its 35th year, and the rare science competition that asks students to reason
forward rather than report backward.** Teams pick a current technology and project it twenty
years into the future, grounding the projection in real science and a literature review.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Scientific Research · Technology Forecasting |
| **Who it's for** | Grades K–12, in four bands: **K–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12**, set by the oldest team member. Teams of two to four |
| **What students produce** | A written technology projection with a bibliography, plus a set of web-page storyboards presenting the future technology to a general audience |
| **What it builds** | Literature review, evidence-based extrapolation, and the discipline of separating what is known from what is plausible — the core move in any research proposal |
| **Recognition** | National first-place team members each receive a **$10,000 U.S. Series EE savings bond at maturity**; second place, **$5,000** each. National winners travel to a Washington, D.C. awards weekend with parents and their coach, expenses paid. Regional winners also receive awards |
| **Key dates** | **Submission deadline: 27 January 2027, 8:00 PM ET** |
| **Investment** | **Free** |
| **Where a mentor fits** | A coach is required — projects are submitted by the coach on the team's behalf, making this a natural fit for a mentored team |
| **Where it leads** | The same competition's 10–12 band, with the same artifact and the same judging standard |
| **Official site** | https://www.exploravision.org/ |

### 7. Thermo Fisher Scientific Junior Innovators Challenge

**The premier middle school science research competition in the United States, and a program
a student cannot simply enter.** Nomination requires placing in the top 10% of sixth through
eighth graders at a Society-affiliated science fair — the same fair network that qualifies
students for ISEF.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Scientific Research |
| **Who it's for** | Grades 6, 7 and 8, by nomination following competition at a Society for Science–affiliated fair in the U.S. or a U.S. territory |
| **What students produce** | A completed science fair research project, then a full online application; finalists compete in team-based engineering and science challenges |
| **What it builds** | Experimental design and data interpretation at a level judged by working scientists, plus the collaborative problem-solving the finals demand |
| **Recognition** | Highly selective: nominees narrow to a Top 300, then **30 finalists**. Top award is the **$25,000 Thermo Fisher ASCEND Award**; category first and second places carry $3,500 and $2,500 toward educational experiences plus an iPad; **every finalist receives $500**, from a prize pool exceeding $100,000 |
| **Key dates** | Governed by the affiliated fair calendar, which runs autumn through spring; the JIC application follows nomination |
| **Investment** | No entry fee for JIC itself; affiliated fairs charge little or nothing. Finalist travel is covered |
| **Where a mentor fits** | Mentored research is expected, following the affiliated-fair convention that adult and institutional contributions are disclosed |
| **Where it leads** | **The strongest continuation in this guide.** The same organization runs Regeneron ISEF and the Regeneron Science Talent Search, and the affiliated-fair route a JIC nominee already knows is the ISEF qualification route |
| **Official site** | https://www.societyforscience.org/jic/ |

### 8. 3M Young Scientist Challenge

**The competition whose prize is a summer working alongside a corporate research scientist.**
Entry is a short video; what follows for the ten finalists is a genuine mentored product
development cycle.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Invention & Product Design · Scientific Research |
| **Who it's for** | Grades 5–8, entering individually |
| **What students produce** | A one-to-two-minute video describing an original invention that solves an everyday problem. The ten finalists then build a working prototype over the summer |
| **What it builds** | The ability to state a problem and a solution compellingly in two minutes, followed — for finalists — by real prototype development under professional supervision |
| **Recognition** | Ten national finalists; the winner takes **$25,000** and the title of America's Top Young Scientist. The finalist mentorship with a 3M scientist is itself among the most substantive awards available to a middle schooler |
| **Key dates** | The entry window runs from **December through April** each season |
| **Investment** | **Free** |
| **Where a mentor fits** | Each finalist is formally paired with a 3M scientist for the summer prototype assignment — mentorship is the award |
| **Where it leads** | No 3M program at 9–12, but the video-pitch-then-prototype sequence maps directly onto the Conrad Challenge and Invention Convention at high school, and finished prototypes frequently become ISEF-affiliated fair projects |
| **Official site** | https://youngscientistlab.com/annual-challenge/about-the-challenge/ |

### 9. NASA TechRise Student Challenge

**Sixty student teams a year design an experiment, build it, and watch it fly.** Very few
competitions at any age end with the student's own hardware leaving the ground.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Aerospace & Space Science · Engineering |
| **Who it's for** | Grades 6–12. Minimum four students per team plus a team lead; all team members must attend the same U.S. public, private or charter school |
| **What students produce** | First an experiment proposal; then, for winning teams, the actual flight hardware, built into a NASA-supplied flight box |
| **What it builds** | Experimental design against real engineering constraints — mass, power, volume, and a launch date that does not move |
| **Recognition** | **60 winning teams** are selected. Each receives **$1,500** to build the experiment, a starter kit including the flight box, and an assigned slot on a NASA-sponsored high-altitude balloon flight |
| **Key dates** | **Proposals due 2 November 2026, 11:59 PM PT.** Completed experiments must reach Future Engineers by **19 May 2027**, flying in **summer 2027** |
| **Investment** | **Free** — winning teams are funded, not charged |
| **Where a mentor fits** | An educator team lead is required, and NASA technical support accompanies the build phase |
| **Where it leads** | The same program covers 9–12, and the build phase is a genuine engineering apprenticeship |
| **Official site** | https://www.futureengineers.org/nasatechrise |

### 10. Genes in Space

**The winning experiment is carried out by astronauts aboard the International Space
Station.** Students design real molecular biology, and finalists refine their proposals with
working scientists before the final judging.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Aerospace & Space Science · Biotechnology |
| **Who it's for** | Grades 7–12, attending a U.S. public, private or home school. Individuals or teams |
| **What students produce** | A written proposal for a biotechnology experiment that could only be answered in microgravity |
| **What it builds** | Hypothesis construction and experimental design in molecular biology, and the specific skill of writing a proposal that a scientific panel will fund |
| **Recognition** | Five finalist teams are selected and paired with scientist mentors; the winning experiment **flies to the ISS and is performed in orbit**, with results presented at the ISS Research and Development Conference |
| **Key dates** | The annual submission deadline falls in **April** |
| **Investment** | **Free** |
| **Where a mentor fits** | Every team must have an adult sponsor; finalists receive formal scientist mentorship to develop the proposal further |
| **Where it leads** | The 7–12 band means no handoff is needed, and the proposal artifact is precisely what ISEF, JSHS and the Science Talent Search reward |
| **Official site** | https://www.genesinspace.org/ |

### 12. Future City

**Four deliverables, one city, and an engineer mentor built into the team structure.** For
the cost of a school lunch, a class gets a season-long engineering design project with a
national final in Washington, D.C.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | Robotics & Engineering · Urban Design |
| **Who it's for** | Grades 6–8 |
| **What students produce** | Four distinct artifacts: a written essay, a physical scale model, a project plan, and a live presentation defended before judges |
| **What it builds** | Systems thinking — students must reconcile infrastructure, environment, budget and human need in a single design — plus the project management that four parallel deliverables demand |
| **Recognition** | Regional competitions in January feed a national final in Washington, D.C. Regional winners receive airfare and hotel for five: three presenting students, the educator **and the mentor** |
| **Key dates** | 2026–27 theme: **Fire Resilient Future** — engineer an urban environment that prevents, withstands and recovers from wildfire. Registration closes in **late October 2026**, by region; regional competitions are held in January |
| **Investment** | **$25 per organization, covering every team that organization enters**, plus a **$100 cap on model materials** and regional travel. The most cost-efficient serious competition in this guide |
| **Where a mentor fits** | An engineer mentor is part of the standard team structure and is one of the five people whose travel to the national final is funded |
| **Where it leads** | **Future City High School**, grades 9–12 — teams of three to five with an adult coach, building a digital city model in Revit alongside an essay, judged in a virtual qualification round with the top 20 advancing to Washington, D.C. |
| **Official site** | https://futurecity.org/ · High school: https://futurecity.org/future-city-high-school/ |

## Choosing well

Three patterns are worth carrying into any conversation with a family.

**Registration and submission are rarely the same date, and registration is the one students
miss.** The Congressional App Challenge opens in May and closes in October. NASA TechRise
takes proposals in November and flight hardware the following May. The entire ISEF pathway
requires approval paperwork completed *before* research begins — months ahead of any fair.
A student who finds a competition in March has already missed most of the year's best ones.

**The strongest mentoring fits are explicit about it.** MIT THINK awards mentorship as the
prize. Lemelson-MIT names mentors in the funded unit. The Junior Academy assigns one. The
JFK essay contest requires a named teacher to read the draft. Future City funds the mentor's
travel to the national final. These are the programmes built for the way BlueRobins works.

**Cost is rarely the headline number.** Future City costs $25 per organization. eCYBERMISSION,
JSHS, the World Food Prize and the Science Talent Search are free. The expensive commitments
— FIRST, Conrad, Invention Convention Nationals, GENIUS Olympiad — mostly charge as a
student advances, which is exactly when a family should already know it is coming.
